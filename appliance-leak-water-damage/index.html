Appliance Leak Water Damage in The Hammocks: What to Know

Nearly every appliance leak we handle comes from one of three sources. When the supply hose on a washing machine splits, water flows under full pressure until somebody turns it off. A dishwasher tends to leak slowly from a door seal or drain hose, out of sight under the cabinets. A refrigerator ice maker line is a thin tube that can drip behind the unit for months. No two of these call for the same cleanup.

For homeowners in The Hammocks, we begin by sliding the machine out to see how far the water traveled. Kitchen leaks tend to end up beneath the cabinets and underneath the floor. In laundry rooms it means the wall behind the machine and the rooms next door, or the ceiling below if the laundry is upstairs. We extract what is standing, measure what has soaked in, and set drying equipment sized to the wet area.

First Five Minutes

Your First Steps

  1. Shut the Machine DownCancel the cycle, and pull the plug only when the floor and the outlet near it are dry. When either one is wet, turn the power off at the breaker.
  2. Shut Off the Water LineFor a washing machine, close both the cold and hot valves on the wall at its back. Look below the sink for the valve that feeds a dishwasher. The small valve for an ice maker line sits behind the refrigerator or underneath.
  3. Soak Up Standing WaterMop or towel up what you can reach so it stops spreading under cabinets and flooring. Use a vacuum only if it is a wet and dry model.
  4. Leave the Door ShutA dishwasher or washer that still holds water should stay closed. Opening the door mid-cycle can release the whole load of water onto the floor.
  5. Document What FailedPhotograph the failed fitting, valve or hose, and hold on to the part itself. It may be requested by the manufacturer of the appliance and by your insurance company.

Coverage

What About Insurance?

A hose that bursts or a machine that overflows without warning is the kind of sudden and accidental event homeowners policies often cover. The policy typically pays for the water damage, not for the appliance or the failed part. A claim is harder when an ice maker line or dishwasher seeped slowly, since coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks. Certain insurers will want to know the hose's age and whether it was kept up. Before cleanup, take photos, save the failed part and read your policy.

Cost

What Drives the Price

  • Type of water, since gray discharge requires sanitizing and clean supply water does not.
  • The time that passed before anyone found the leak, which matters most for slow drips at the back of a unit.
  • The appliance involved, and if the leak was a slow seep or a fast flood.
  • Presence of mold, which adds removal work in front of the drying.

Why It Happens

What Causes It

  • A kinked or cracked ice maker line, or one whose fitting has worked loose.
  • A washer drain hose that slips out of the standpipe, or a clogged standpipe.
  • Too much detergent or an overloaded drum that makes the machine overflow.
  • Installation mistakes like a loose supply line connection or a dishwasher that does not sit level.
  • Water under a refrigerator from a blocked defrost drain or a drain pan with a crack in it.
If It Is Left

What the Water Damages

Baseboards and Wall Bottoms

Water standing on a laundry or kitchen floor wicks up into baseboards and the bottom of the drywall. The wall behind the appliance is often the wettest because nobody could see it.

Hidden Mold at the Back

The warm, dark space behind a refrigerator or dishwasher rarely gets checked. Mold on the floor and wall can be fed by a slow drip in that spot well before it is noticed.

Floors and What Is Under Them

Water slides beneath laminate, vinyl plank and hardwood. Boards cup or swell, and the subfloor underneath stays wet long after the surface looks dry.

Kitchen Cabinets

Dishwasher leaks soak cabinet sides and toe kicks from underneath. Swelling and separation follow, and for weeks the soaked cabinet boxes may trap moisture against the wall.
